## The Information Bottleneck Problem

The module explained that the brain receives approximately:
- 11 million bits of sensory information per second
- while conscious awareness processes only about 50 bits per second

This creates a major information bottleneck problem that requires filtering mechanisms. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

The AI comparison was extremely important because transformer models face the exact same challenge:
- too much input
- limited processing capacity
- need for selective prioritization

The module showed that biological attention and transformer attention solve similar computational problems.

## Types of Attention

The coursework introduced three major forms of attention:

### Sustained Attention
Maintaining focus over extended periods.

Example:
- studying
- monitoring systems
- air traffic control

### Selective Attention
Focusing on one stimulus while suppressing distractions.

Example:
- studying in a noisy environment
- listening during conversations

### Divided Attention
Attempting to manage multiple tasks simultaneously.

The module emphasized that true multitasking is mostly a myth and that humans rapidly switch tasks rather than truly processing them simultaneously. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

## Cocktail Party Effect

One of the most interesting concepts was the Cocktail Party Effect, where humans can focus on one conversation in noisy environments while still subconsciously monitoring other stimuli. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

The module explained that:
- the brain monitors background information unconsciously
- personally relevant stimuli can instantly capture attention
- attention operates on multiple levels simultaneously

This concept strongly resembled how transformer systems dynamically shift attention weights toward relevant tokens.

## Inattentional Blindness and the Invisible Gorilla Experiment

The module explored the famous invisible gorilla experiment by Simons & Chabris (1999). Participants focused on counting basketball passes while a person in a gorilla suit walked directly through the scene. Approximately half of participants never noticed the gorilla. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

This demonstrated that:
- seeing is not the same as noticing
- consciousness depends on attention
- unattended information may never reach awareness

This concept was important because it demonstrated how both biological and AI systems selectively prioritize information.

## Bottom-Up vs Top-Down Attention

The module introduced two major modes of attention:

### Bottom-Up Attention
Automatic, stimulus-driven attention triggered by:
- loud sounds
- flashes of light
- sudden movement
- unexpected stimuli

### Top-Down Attention
Goal-driven voluntary attention based on current objectives.

Examples:
- searching for a car
- reading
- studying
- searching for information

:contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

This distinction helped explain how AI attention systems can prioritize either:
- salient inputs
- or goal-directed contextual relevance

## The Brain’s Three Attention Networks

The coursework explored Michael Posner’s three-network model of attention:

### Alerting Network
The brain’s alarm system.

Key regions:
- locus coeruleus
- thalamus

Function:
- maintaining readiness
- detecting potential events

### Orienting Network
Controls shifts in attentional focus.

Key regions:
- parietal cortex
- superior colliculus

Function:
- directing attention toward stimuli

### Executive Network
The brain’s control center.

Key regions:
- prefrontal cortex
- anterior cingulate cortex

Function:
- conflict resolution
- voluntary focus
- impulse suppression
- decision-making

:contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

## The Biased Competition Model

The module introduced the biased competition model developed by Desimone and Duncan.

The concept explained that:
- neural representations compete for processing resources
- attention biases the competition
- winning signals are amplified
- losing signals are suppressed

:contentReference[oaicite:10]{index=10}

This was directly connected to:
- softmax functions
- transformer attention weights
- neural competition mechanisms in AI
