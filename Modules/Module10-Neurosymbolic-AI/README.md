## Key Concepts Learned

One of the biggest lessons from this module was understanding that intelligence has two major components:

1. Neural intelligence:
   - fast
   - automatic
   - pattern-based
   - excellent at perception

2. Symbolic intelligence:
   - slower
   - logical
   - rule-based
   - explainable

The module explained that the human brain uses both systems together naturally, while most AI systems currently specialize in only one. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

The module compared the strengths and weaknesses of neural networks and symbolic AI systems.

### Neural Networks
Strengths:
- image recognition
- language generation
- speech recognition
- pattern detection
- autonomous gameplay

Weaknesses:
- black box decisions
- adversarial attacks
- lack of explainability
- poor logical reasoning
- high data requirements

- logic
- explainability

Weaknesses:

## The Symbol Grounding Problem

One important concept was the symbol grounding problem.

The module explained that symbolic systems manipulate labels and rules, but symbols like “red” or “chair” have no direct sensory meaning unless connected to real-world experience.

Neural systems solve grounding through perception, while symbolic systems solve reasoning through logic. Neuro-symbolic AI attempts to combine both strengths into a unified architecture. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

## Neuro-Symbolic Integration Strategies

The module introduced four integration strategies:

1. Sequential
   - neural perception → symbolic reasoning

2. Guided
   - symbolic rules constrain neural learning

3. Compiled
   - symbolic rules embedded into neural weights

4. Bidirectional
   - continuous feedback between symbolic and neural systems

This helped explain how modern hybrid AI systems are designed. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

## Global Workspace Theory

Another major concept was Global Workspace Theory.

The module described how specialized brain systems compete for access to a global workspace that broadcasts important information throughout the brain.

This concept is important for AI because future AGI systems may require:
- specialized neural modules
- shared symbolic memory
- reasoning coordination
- attention mechanisms

The module explained how this could become a blueprint for future intelligent systems. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

## AGI and Future AI Research

The module explored one of the biggest debates in artificial intelligence:

Is scaling deep learning enough for AGI?

Some researchers believe larger neural models will eventually achieve AGI, while others argue that reasoning, causality, grounding, and symbolic processing are still missing.

The module introduced Yann LeCun’s JEPA architecture as an alternative direction focused on world models and physical understanding rather than language prediction alone. :contentReference[oaicite:9]{index=9}
